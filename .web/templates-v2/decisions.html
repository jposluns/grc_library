Accountability & decision rights

§01

Who owns this decision, and where is that written?

A decision with no named owner still gets made: by a supplier’s default, an engineer’s judgement call, or nobody, until an incident assigns it in hindsight. The governing body remains accountable either way.

What the corpus offers: it fixes where each governance authority sits (in charters, a role-authority register, and decision rules) so a decision cannot fall between roles. Published leadership pages:

Cybersecurity & operational resilience

§02

Could we take the hit and keep operating?

Preventing every incident is not a plausible plan; withstanding one is. The exposure leadership owns is the gap between the controls that exist on paper and the organization’s tested ability to absorb an identity-provider compromise, a compromised release-signing key, or the loss of a critical service, and keep operating.

What the corpus offers: protective controls plus continuity, recovery, impact analysis, and incident coordination documents, and scenarios that walk leadership through the bad day itself. Published leadership pages:

  • ScenarioWhen the enterprise identity provider can no longer be trusted↗
  • ScenarioWhen a release-signing key is compromised after software reaches production↗
Security & risk (CISO): continuity, recovery & incidents Compliance & evidence: control testing & audit readiness Technology: operational security & monitoring

Data, privacy & the information lifecycle

§03

Do we control our information from creation to destruction?

Information the organization cannot classify, locate, or provably destroy is a liability with no expiry date. Cross-border movement and changing legal boundaries turn quiet data flows into board-level exposure without any system changing at all.

What the corpus offers: governance of personal and sensitive information across its whole life: classification, cross-border flows, retention, and evidenced destruction. Published leadership pages:

  • Executive BriefWhat the governing body should require for AI data governance and classification↗
  • ScenarioWhen a customer-data service must move after its legal operating boundary changes↗
  • JourneyFrom retention rule to destruction evidence: the governed journey of a record↗
Security & risk (CISO): classification & handling controls Compliance & evidence: privacy & data-protection obligations Technology: data architecture

Supplier & cloud dependency

§04

How exposed are we through the parties we depend on?

Most organizations now run on other people’s infrastructure, software, and models. The exposure is concentration you have not measured, exits you have not rehearsed, and assurance you have taken on a supplier’s word, discovered, typically, on the day a critical supplier fails.

What the corpus offers: concentration, exit, ongoing monitoring, and assurance over third parties, through to fourth- and nth-party risk. Published leadership pages:

  • Executive BriefWhat the governing body should require for third-party and AI supply-chain risk oversight↗
  • ScenarioWhat happens when a critical AI supplier fails↗
Security & risk (CISO): third-party & supply-chain controls Compliance & evidence: regulatory mapping & assurance Technology: software composition & pipeline controls

Compliance & assurance

§05

Can we show, with evidence, that our controls operate?

“We have a policy” is an assertion. What an auditor, a regulator, or a court asks for is evidence that the control the policy requires actually operated, on the days it mattered. The exposure is the distance between the two.

What the corpus offers: controls mapped to obligations, a control-testing discipline, and evidence structures an assurer can rely on. Published leadership pages:

  • Outcome MapHow leadership can know, with evidence, whether the controls it relies on are operating↗
  • JourneyFrom policy intent to independent assurance: the journey of a privileged-access control↗
Security & risk (CISO): assurance map & risk reporting Compliance & evidence: control testing & audit packages Technology: monitoring & change control

Risk acceptance & exceptions

§06

Which risks have we knowingly accepted, and who signed?

Every organization accepts risk; the question is whether it does so on purpose. Silent exceptions (the control that was waived and never revisited) surface in the post-incident review, attached to no name and no expiry date.

What the corpus offers: defensible, time-bound risk acceptance with named owners: an exception and risk-acceptance policy and the acceptance procedure behind it. Published leadership page:

  • Executive BriefWhat the governing body should require before an AI system goes into production↗
Security & risk (CISO): risk acceptance procedure Compliance & evidence: exception & acceptance policy Technology: recorded design trade-offs

Enterprise architecture & technology debt

§07

What is our accumulated technology debt costing us?

Deferred modernization compounds quietly: each postponement is individually rational and collectively a balance-sheet exposure no one owns. Leadership finds out what the debt cost when a change that should take weeks takes quarters, or when the unsupported component fails.

What the corpus offers: architecture decisions tied to governance (decision records, review procedures, a technology radar), so deferred work is a visible, owned exposure. Published leadership page:

  • Oversight Question SetWhat the governing body should ask about enterprise architecture and accumulated technology debt↗
Security & risk (CISO): vulnerability & patch exposure Compliance & evidence: exception governance Technology: reference architectures & ADRs

Workforce, conduct & operating model

§08

Do the people running the controls have the mandate to run them?

Controls are run by people: screened, trained, empowered people, or not. The exposure is an operating model where responsibility is assigned to roles that lack the authority, the headcount, or the reporting line to act on it.

What the corpus offers: people, roles, screening, conduct, whistleblower routes, and the operating model that runs the controls day to day. Published leadership page:

  • Executive BriefWhat the governing body should require for workforce, conduct, and operating-model oversight↗
Security & risk (CISO): personnel security & insider risk Compliance & evidence: conduct & reporting frameworks Technology: the operational service model

AI & autonomous systems

§09

What is AI doing on our behalf, and who approved it?

AI is one exposure among the ten: governed like the rest, which is precisely the point. The questions are the familiar governance questions asked of a new class of system: what is deployed, on whose approval, under whose oversight, with what authority, and what happens when it acts beyond it.

What the corpus offers: AI governed from intake through production approval, oversight, incidents, and retirement. This is currently the concern with the deepest published narrative coverage:

Programme establishment & maturity

§10

Where does our programme stand, and what would better look like?

A governance programme is itself something to govern: it has a current state, a target state, and a path between them that someone must own. Without an honest maturity picture, investment goes where the last incident pointed.
