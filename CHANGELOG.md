# Changelog

All notable changes to this repository are recorded in this file in two forms: while a week is current, one compact entry per change (a `date | version | PR` header followed by a short, plain-language summary a general reader can follow), or a daily roll-up summarizing a day's changes under a date and contiguous PR range; once a week completes, its entries are rolled up into a single weekly summary carrying the week's contiguous PR range, so the root file preserves complete PR coverage while staying scannable as the entry count grows. The full maintainer-grade detail for each change (the Added / Changed / Removed / Fixed / Security / Verification sections) is kept in the private sibling working-state store and in git history; that mirror is how this project's maintainer tracks the full audit trail. The convention is project-specific; forks may keep their own working-state store and adopt their own approach to detailed change tracking. The mechanics are documented in the [`change-tracking` governance rule](guardrails/governance/change-tracking.md). Significant project milestones are recorded inline as dated `MILESTONE:` entries (`**YYYY-MM-DD | MILESTONE:** ...`) that float to the top of the entry stream, above that day's per-PR entries; those are never condensed by the daily or weekly roll-ups, and the roll-up tooling skips them by their `MILESTONE:` marker.
The format follows [Keep a Changelog](https://keepachangelog.com/en/1.1.0/) loosely; individual document versions follow semantic versioning as defined in [`specification-ingestion.md`](specification-ingestion.md). The library as a whole carries a Calendar Versioning (CalVer) version of the form `YYYY.MM.patch`; see [`specification-master-project.md`](specification-master-project.md) section 4.5.

**2026-10-15 | 2026.10.1483 | PR #2781** - security: cryptographic erasure safeguards. Destroying encryption keys to erase data now requires every backed-up or escrowed key copy to be accounted for and destroyed, including on drives and devices. For Restricted data, the Chief Information Security Officer (CISO) judges whether it needs long-term confidentiality. If so, the CISO must confirm the encryption will stay strong enough; this is recorded when expired records are destroyed. Without that confirmation or another method, the data is kept as recorded extra retention, needing Legal Counsel's written approval beyond seven years. This matters because erased data stays encrypted and could be read if encryption weakens.

**2026-10-15 | 2026.10.1482 | PR #2780** - operations: IT operations must now tell the security operations centre (SOC) immediately when the most severe priority is declared for any complete service outage. The SOC can then judge whether a security incident caused it, or is suspected to have caused it. The incident escalation guidance already asked the SOC to make this judgement, but no document said how the SOC would learn of the outage. No existing duty covered this, so one was added. This matters because outages that may have a security cause now reach the people who assess them.

**2026-10-15 | 2026.10.1481 | PR #2779** - security: the post-incident review for an incident at the two lowest security severity levels is now conducted by the Incident Commander where one was assigned, and otherwise by the security operations centre (SOC) lead. Before, the SOC lead conducted it whether or not an Incident Commander was assigned. The procedure's review rule and all three summaries of the SOC lead's duties now say this. Reviews at the two most severe levels stay with the Incident Commander. This matters because it settles who owns these reviews, and all three documents give the same answer.

**2026-10-15 | 2026.10.1480 | PR #2778** - security: outage trigger in incident response. The security incident response procedure now treats a complete service outage caused, or suspected to be caused, by a security incident as reaching the most severe security level, using the escalation matrix's meaning of "security incident". This aligns the procedure with the escalation matrix and the IT service management framework, so all three now handle such an outage consistently. The procedure and the IT service management framework now list the escalation matrix as a related document.

**2026-10-15 | 2026.10.1479 | PR #2777** - security: eight device, media and recovery documents now name who confirms, ahead of a wipe, rebuild or disposal, whether an investigation treats a device, its user or its data as evidence before an Incident Commander is assigned. For the two most severe security levels, it is the Chief Information Security Officer or a duty delegate authorized in advance. At those levels, checks for keys, records and other data still wait for an Incident Commander or Legal Counsel, pending a decision. This matters because evidence decisions about devices in a serious incident have a named owner from the start.

**2026-10-15 | 2026.10.1478 | PR #2776** - security: the three summaries of the security operations centre (SOC) lead's duties now match. Each gives the SOC lead only the authority and duties set out in the incident response procedure and records retention standard, and no other Incident Commander authority. These apply only while an incident at the two lowest security severity levels has no Incident Commander, except any post-incident review, which the SOC lead conducts with or without an Incident Commander. No duty changes. This matters because reviews kept finding a duty missing from one summary, so readers could get different answers from different documents.

**2026-10-15 | 2026.10.1477 | PR #2775** - tooling: the reminder that a day's change-log summary is due now says to produce it with the dedicated tool in the private maintainers' repository, which writes the public summary and its detailed copy together. It also warns that a summary written by hand fails the pre-push check that the two entries' headings match. This matters because, on the same day, a hand-written summary based on the old reminder was blocked by that check. The reminder still never blocks a push, and a new self-test fails if the new wording is removed.

**2026-10-15 | 2026.10.1476 | PR #2774** - security: cryptographic erasure, which makes data unreadable by erasing every copy of its encryption keys, stays the accepted way to destroy digital data on logical or cloud storage under NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 2. The encrypted data stays on the storage, so it is protected only while the encryption stays strong. For long-lived Restricted data, cryptographic erasure is used only where the organization has confirmed the encryption is expected to stay adequate for as long as the data stays sensitive; otherwise another method is used. This matters because future encryption weaknesses or quantum computing could make that data recoverable.

**Week of 2026-05-25 (PRs #1-#2)** This two-entry week marks the start of the library's public life. The Governance, Risk, and Compliance Documentation Library had its first release, under the Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0 International (CC BY-SA 4.0) licence, alongside an internal corpus-wide hyperlink sweep and backlog cleanup. The corpus itself is far older: it grew out of practitioner work in the early 2000s and was formalized around 2009 by the original maintainer together with a large group of friends and colleagues, a team of roughly ten regulars who wrote, edited, and peer-reviewed every document by hand. This week also closes a transition many months in the making: the shift from that hand-tended, peer-reviewed corpus to an AI-managed one in which a Human In The Loop (HITL) is required: a person who authorizes, verifies, and signs off on the machine's work.

The team's ambition extends past their own library. As they adopt AI tooling, they want the lessons from that move to help everyone else: the suffering through an AI that, left without guardrails, will lie, invent citations, and misrepresent its own finished work, and the discipline it takes to catch every such error before it reaches the corpus. Every discipline they learn is distilled into a portable [replicable pack of rules](guardrails/README.md), tools, and reference material, so that anyone facing the same task of keeping a governance corpus accurate, citable, and trustworthy can pick it up and build on their experience. They are excited to do this in the open, on GitHub, and expect the beginning to be messy. That is the point: to watch how unruly it starts, then use an ever-growing lattice of guardrails, audit gates, commit hooks, and linters working quietly in the background to make it steadily more standard, more methodical, and finally simple, shifting as much of the quality-assurance burden as possible onto machinery that never tires and never forgets.
