# Changelog

All notable changes to this repository are recorded in this file as one compact entry per change: a `date | version | PR` header followed by a short, plain-language summary a general reader can follow. The full maintainer-grade detail for each change (the Added / Changed / Removed / Fixed / Security / Verification sections) is kept in the private sibling working-state store and in git history; that mirror is how this project's maintainer tracks the full audit trail. The convention is project-specific; forks may keep their own working-state store and adopt their own approach to detailed change tracking. The mechanics are documented in the [`change-tracking` governance rule](dev-security/claude-rules/governance/change-tracking.md). Significant project milestones are additionally recorded as persistent dated entries under the `## Milestones` heading; those are never condensed by the daily or weekly roll-ups.
The format follows [Keep a Changelog](https://keepachangelog.com/en/1.1.0/) loosely; individual document versions follow semantic versioning as defined in [`specification-ingestion.md`](specification-ingestion.md). The library as a whole carries a Calendar Versioning (CalVer) version of the form `YYYY.MM.patch`; see [`specification-master-project.md`](specification-master-project.md) section 4.5.

## Milestones

### 2026-07-29: Public and private separated, a cleaner library for adopters

The GRC Library now draws a clear line between what an adopter needs and what belongs to the project's own development. Everything in the public repository is what you clone, run, and customize: the corpus, the audit gates, the governance rule pack, and the commands that operate them. The internal development apparatus, the working-state records, the orchestration and QA tooling, the session and metrics ledgers, and the operational runbooks, moves to a private companion repository. It lives there because it exists to make our own development and the adoption path cleaner and more optimized, not because an adopter needs it to use the library.

The move addresses a concrete problem. Too many tools in the public tree applied only to our internal process, and their presence caused confusion about what adopters were actually expected to run. The working-state records have already moved to the private repository, and the internal tooling is following. The direction is a focused public library and a set of gates that stand on their own, with the internal development detail kept where it belongs rather than left in the public tree to explain away.

The same separation covers human-in-the-loop (HITL) processes that carry personal information. Any workflow that records inter-team-member communication or other personal data is expanded and kept in the private repository, so that private conversation stays private and personal information is never disseminated through the public corpus. That private conversation is also, frequently, unsparing about the quality of AI-generated writing, one of the forces that drove Jeff to create the Clean Language project at [cleanlanguage.ai](https://cleanlanguage.ai), built from his [`jposluns/ai-language`](https://github.com/jposluns/ai-language) repository.

Alongside the split, the project's core instruction file (CLAUDE.md) begins a right-sizing. Moving adopter-irrelevant operational detail into the private repository is the first step. The direction is a smaller instruction core that loads fewer tokens on every task, with the material specific to a given process or task loaded when that work is actually underway rather than carried at all times. A leaner core is a faster and cheaper working baseline for every session, and it keeps the public instructions focused on what an adopter reads to understand and extend the library.

**Week of 2026-05-25 (versions 2026.05.136 to 2026.05.137)**

This two-entry week marks not the start of the work but the start of its public life: the first release of the Governance, Risk, and Compliance Documentation Library under the Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0 International (CC BY-SA 4.0) licence, alongside an internal corpus-wide hyperlink sweep and backlog cleanup. The corpus itself is far older. It grew out of practitioner work in the early 2000s and was formalized around 2009 by the original maintainer together with a large group of friends and colleagues, a team of roughly ten regulars who wrote, edited, and peer-reviewed every document by hand. This week also closes a transition many months in the making: the shift from that hand-tended, peer-reviewed corpus to an AI-managed one in which a Human In The Loop (HITL) is not optional but required, a person who authorizes, verifies, and signs off on the machine's work rather than trusting it.

The team's ambition reaches well past their own library. As they adopt AI tooling, they want the hard-won lessons of that move to be useful to everyone: the suffering through an AI that, left without guardrails, will lie, invent citations, and misrepresent its own finished work, and the discipline it takes to catch every such error before it reaches the corpus. Every discipline they learn is being distilled into a portable, [replicable pack of rules](dev-security/claude-rules/README.md), tools, and reference material, so that anyone facing the same task of keeping a governance corpus accurate, citable, and trustworthy can pick it up and build on their experience. They are genuinely excited to do this in the open, on GitHub, and they expect the beginning to be messy. That is the whole point: to watch how unruly it starts, and then, with an ever-growing lattice of guardrails, audit gates, commit hooks, and linters working quietly in the background, make it steadily more standard, more methodical, and finally simple, moving as much of the quality-assurance burden as possible onto machinery that never tires and never forgets.
