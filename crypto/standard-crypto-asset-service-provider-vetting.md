## 1. Purpose

This standard sets the crypto-specific acceptance requirements for selecting and onboarding an external provider through which the organization trades, exchanges, transfers, safeguards, or obtains access to crypto-assets. It is a requirements overlay under the [Crypto-Asset Governance Framework](framework-crypto-asset-governance.md), implemented through the organization's existing supplier lifecycle (intake, tiering, due diligence, contracting, monitoring, and exit), and it is not a substitute for the authorization, procurement, legal, security, privacy, or financial-crime review that applicable law and internal policy require.

"Crypto-asset service provider" is the organization-neutral umbrella term for such a provider; "CASP" is used only where the EU Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2023/1114, MiCA) is the applicable regime. A documented, evidence-backed decision precedes opening an account, depositing assets or funds, routing transactions, provisioning an API or credential, or otherwise relying operationally on a provider.

## 2. Applicability

This standard applies before initial engagement, and again upon a material change in legal entity, ownership, authorization, service scope, custody model, supported network, or outsourcing chain, to an external legal person providing one or more of: custody or administration of crypto-assets; a crypto-asset trading platform or exchange; exchange of crypto-assets for funds or other crypto-assets; reception, transmission, execution, or routing of orders; crypto-asset transfer services; on-ramp or off-ramp facilities and any related payment provider; or sub-custody or another material outsourced component of those services.

It does not govern selection of the underlying blockchain or distributed-ledger network (the framework's platform and technology model), the organization's internal custody mechanics (the [Digital Asset Custody Standard](standard-digital-asset-custody.md)), or token classification (the framework). A decentralized protocol with no accountable contracting provider is out of scope unless the maintainer expands it; where there is a contracting provider, that provider is in scope.

## 3. Requirements

### 3.1 Engagement definition and inherent-risk record

Before provider evidence is requested, the relationship owner records the business purpose and accountable owner; the exact legal entity and platform names; the requested service types; the jurisdictions from and into which services are supplied; the target user population; the crypto-assets and networks involved; whether the provider controls assets, keys, credentials, client funds, order routing, or settlement; the data, API, account, and system integrations; the expected volume and operational criticality; the affiliated entities, banking or payment partners, custodians, and delegates; and the exit and substitution constraints. The asset classification and activity approval required by the framework precede provider activation, and the supplier tier and risk score are assigned through the [Third-Party and Supply Chain Risk Standard](../risk/standard-third-party-and-supply-chain-risk.md) rather than a parallel crypto tier.

### 3.2 Legal identity and authorization gate

The contracting and operating entity is verified against authoritative corporate and regulatory records: legal name, registration or Legal Entity Identifier where available, registered office, approved trading names, and website or application ownership. For each service-and-jurisdiction pair, the organization determines whether a licence, registration, notification, exemption, or other formal permission is required; verifies the provider through the applicable official register or competent authority; and records the regulator, authorization identifier, permitted services, status, restrictions, and verification date. Under MiCA the authorization and notification routes are Articles 59 and 60 and the public register is Article 109; exact article and RTS mapping is held in the [MiCA Implementation Annex](../compliance/financial-services/annex-mica-implementation.md). A missing required authorization, an identity mismatch, or an intended service outside the provider's permission is a mandatory stop. Whether a provider that is lawfully unregulated for the relevant service is nonetheless acceptable is an organizational risk-appetite decision, not a universal prohibition this standard imposes.

### 3.3 Ownership, management, governance, and conduct

The organization obtains and assesses the group and ownership structure and any qualifying holders; the management body's competence and repute; the control functions and compliance responsibilities; relevant regulatory-enforcement, authorization-withdrawal, insolvency, or fraud history; and the provider's conflicts-of-interest and complaints arrangements and its governance of client communications, pricing, costs, and fees. These are used as selection evidence, drawing on the provider governance and conduct obligations MiCA sets in Articles 66, 68, 71, and 72 without reproducing the provider-facing procedures those articles impose. Material unresolved governance, integrity, conflict, or enforcement concerns feed the residual-risk decision and may require rejection; a legal prerequisite cannot be waived by an exception.

### 3.4 Financial capacity and prudential evidence

Financial viability is reviewed in proportion to asset exposure and service criticality, including recent audited financial statements where available, regulatory-capital or prudential status where applicable, any own-funds, guarantee, or insurance evidence relied upon, and the assumptions behind it. MiCA states the prudential requirement for CASPs in Article 67; the applicable regulator supplies any legal minimum, and this standard does not copy a capital figure or assert a universal insurance amount or coverage ratio into the reusable core. Insurance alone is not treated as evidence of solvency or of client-asset coverage.

### 3.5 Security, ICT, continuity, and incident readiness

The [Supplier Security and Privacy Assurance Standard](../supply-chain/standard-supplier-security-and-privacy-assurance.md) applies according to the assigned supplier tier, and its contractual security baselines and incident-notification times govern; this standard adds no separate crypto-specific cadence. The crypto overlay additionally requires evidence of the ICT and ledger infrastructure supporting the service; a documented ICT-risk and data-protection control environment; the critical internal and third-party technology dependencies; the security and incident-management procedures; relevant independent audits, vulnerability assessments, and penetration-test evidence; a maintained and tested business-continuity plan, including for functions supplied by third parties; and recovery of transaction, custody, order, and position records.

### 3.6 Service-specific assessment

Where a provider performs more than one service, the applicable criteria apply cumulatively. A trading platform supplies its operating and asset-admission rules, supported assets and networks, and system resilience, capacity, suspension, and delisting controls (MiCA Article 76). A custodian or sub-custodian demonstrates conformance to the [Digital Asset Custody Standard](standard-digital-asset-custody.md): the custody agreement, the custody policy, the register of positions, the safekeeping and segregation of client assets and funds, and the return of assets and of client rights on a ledger change are the custody obligations MiCA sets in Articles 70 and 75, which the custody standard's wallet-tiering, key-approval, and module-assurance controls implement. An order-execution or routing provider supplies its execution policy, the venues relied upon and the selection method, and its conflicts and routing incentives. A transfer provider supplies its supported assets and networks, its initiation and completion model, and its operational-failure and reconciliation controls. A crypto-or-funds exchange or on-ramp or off-ramp provider supplies its non-discriminatory commercial policy and the type of clients and conditions it transacts on, a firm price or a published method for determining price and any applicable transaction limit (MiCA Article 77), the authorization analysis for both the crypto service and any related payment service, and the safeguarding model for client funds. Every network-dependent service identifies its supported chains and confirmation and finality assumptions and its policy for network upgrades, forks, chain splits, airdrops, and asset-rights events. This standard checks the provider's service terms and controls against the network already approved under the framework; it does not re-perform the framework's consensus assessment.

### 3.7 Contract requirements

The agreement or control schedule addresses, as applicable: the exact legal entity, authorized services, jurisdictions, assets, and networks; roles, service levels, and performance indicators; asset, fund, credential, order, and data ownership; security, incident notification, evidence preservation, and cooperation; audit, assurance-report, and regulatory-access rights; fees, pricing method, conflicts, complaints, and material-change notice; custody, segregation, liability, and return of assets or access means; delegates, subcontractors, banking partners, and flow-down requirements; and continuity, recovery, wind-down, termination, transition, and exit assistance, including export of records and transaction and position histories. MiCA fixes the provider's continuing responsibility for outsourced functions in Article 73. Where the provider uses non-negotiable terms, every gap, compensating control, residual risk, and approval is recorded; a regulatory prerequisite or authorization defect is not a waivable contract gap.

### 3.8 Outsourcing, sub-custody, and concentration

The organization obtains a map of the provider's material outsourced functions, delegates and sub-delegates, service locations, and banking or payment partners, and assesses whether the provider remains accountable, retains the expertise and resources to supervise those functions, provides access to service and control information, supports audit and competent-authority cooperation, and can continue, recover, or be substituted if a material sub-provider fails (MiCA Article 73). Dependencies are recorded in the existing supplier and concentration registers; portfolio-level concentration analysis remains with the fourth-party procedure and concentration register, and this standard supplies the crypto-specific dependency facts.

### 3.9 Approval and onboarding gate

The decision record states the services and entities assessed, the evidence reviewed and its verification dates, the inherent and residual risk, any unmet conditions and compensating controls, the decision to approve, approve with conditions, or reject, the approving roles and any exception reference, the reverification requirements, and the post-onboarding monitoring owner and triggers. Candidate participants in the decision are the relationship owner, the supplier-risk function, the Crypto-Asset Governance Approver, Legal or Compliance, Information Security, Finance or Treasury where funds or counterparty exposure is material, and Privacy where personal data is involved; the organization confirms this split against its own role-authority register. No account activation, asset or fund deposit, production integration, credential provision, or transaction routing occurs until the mandatory legal, custody, contract, and residual-risk conditions are met, after which the provider is handed to the existing onboarding procedure for supplier identification, register updates, access provisioning, and monitoring scheduling.

### 3.10 Reverification and lifecycle handoff

Reassessment is required upon an authorization restriction, suspension, withdrawal, or scope change; an ownership, management, or legal-entity change; a material change in services, supported networks, custody, or settlement; the addition or change of a material custodian, delegate, payment partner, or ICT provider; a significant incident, control failure, financial deterioration, or enforcement event; a material contract change or renewal; or exit, transfer, or replacement planning. The ongoing-monitoring and supplier-audit procedures own the cadence and execution; this standard supplies the crypto-specific triggers and the evidence they call for.

## 4. Evidence requirements

Evidence for this standard comprises the engagement definition and service-and-jurisdiction matrix; the legal-entity and official-register verification with its date and the permitted services; the ownership, management, governance, conflicts, complaints, and enforcement review; the financial, prudential, and insurance evidence and the financial-risk assessment; the security and ICT evidence review and the business-continuity plan and its test evidence; the applicable service-specific checklist and findings, including the custody-standard conformance assessment; the outsourcing, sub-custody, banking-partner, and geographic dependency map; the contract or control schedule and any recorded deviations; the risk assessment, treatment plan, conditions, and final decision; the supplier, crypto-domain, and concentration-register entries; and the monitoring schedule, trigger list, exit plan, and accountable owners. Evidence is retained through the corpus records-retention controls rather than a duration named here.

## 5. Limitations

This is original library content and reproduces no external control text; MiCA-grounded requirements are named at the point of use, and an organization under another regime applies its own governing law. The standard is regime-neutral: MiCA and its regulatory technical standard, Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2025/305, are used as a detailed evidence model, not as a claim that every provider or adopter is subject to MiCA, and the lifecycle discipline it overlays is grounded in ISO/IEC 27036-2:2022. Applicable-law analysis determines whether authorization, notification, prudential, payment, conduct, anti-money-laundering, counter-terrorist-financing, or travel-rule obligations attach. The standard does not certify a provider, guarantee solvency, or eliminate counterparty, custody, technology, market, or concentration risk, and it does not replace the underlying blockchain-platform decision, the asset classification, the custody controls, the generic supplier lifecycle, or the jurisdiction annexes. Crypto-specific anti-money-laundering, counter-terrorist-financing, and travel-rule criteria, detailed payment-services authorization mechanics, a standardized proof-of-reserves methodology, and non-EU licensing equivalence are outside this release pending held authoritative sources and future jurisdiction annexes; quantitative thresholds such as capital buffers, insurance minima, or exposure limits require governing-law grounding or an explicit organizational risk-appetite decision.
