## Why this matters

An organization's critical operations carry a dependency on services other organizations run, and an AI capability can reach the organization through a chain of them: a model provider, a platform, a retrieval or orchestration service, and the suppliers behind those suppliers. The [AI board oversight guide](../../ai/guide-ai-board-oversight.md) establishes that accountability for the organization's use of AI rests with the governing body and cannot be delegated to the AI system or to management (its section 2), and its governance red flags treat responsibility attributed to the system or the vendor, rather than an accountable person, as a warning sign (its section 6). A supplier can operate a control on the organization's behalf; it cannot hold the organization's accountability for it.

The governing body does not run supplier assessments and does not sign contracts. Its oversight is of the programme: whether a defined lifecycle governs every supplier relationship, whether AI suppliers receive the additional scrutiny the corpus defines for them, whether the organization can see past its direct suppliers to the dependencies beneath them, and whether all of that is visible in evidence rather than described in assurances. This brief points to where the corpus establishes each of those. The framing of the governing body's role as programme-level oversight is synthesized across the sources named below; see the limitations.

## What the corpus establishes

- **A single standard governs third-party and supply-chain risk end to end.** The [third-party and supply chain risk standard](../../risk/standard-third-party-and-supply-chain-risk.md) requires due diligence before onboarding for the criticality tiers the standard names, at the depth it sets for each, at the depth its criticality tiers set, and its due diligence domains include AI systems in service delivery (its section 4.1). The tiers, domains, and depths live in the standard; this brief does not restate them.
- **Supplier governance is a lifecycle, not an onboarding event.** The [supplier and cloud governance framework](../../supply-chain/framework-supplier-and-cloud-governance.md) defines the stages from intake through exit, and pairs each stage with a required governance activity and the evidence class it produces (its supplier lifecycle section). The evidence class paired with each stage is evidence that the stage operated.
- **AI suppliers carry additional, defined obligations.** The [third-party AI due diligence procedure](../../supply-chain/procedure-third-party-ai-due-diligence.md) requires the pre-engagement checklist items applicable to the supplier's AI classification to be assessed before any AI supplier contract is executed (its section 5) and requires AI-specific contract provisions covering model documentation, explainability commitments, incident notification, deletion on termination, audit rights over AI system logs, and advance notice of model version changes (its section 6). The notification windows, deletion timelines, and retention floors are values the procedure owns; the brief routes to them. The [supplier and cloud governance framework](../../supply-chain/framework-supplier-and-cloud-governance.md) states that AI suppliers require specific assessment, with the coverage it lists stated as what assessment should cover (its AI supplier requirements section). The umbrella standard states its own AI service-provider contract requirements, which overlap with but do not coincide with the procedure's (its section 5.1), and the [supplier security and privacy assurance standard](../../supply-chain/standard-supplier-security-and-privacy-assurance.md) requires minimum security and privacy clauses for suppliers in its scope (its section 6).
- **Foundation models have their own lifecycle gate.** The [foundation model lifecycle procedure](../../ai/procedure-foundation-model-lifecycle.md) defines the required pre-engagement evaluation outputs, from capability and adversarial testing through training-data provenance, substitutability, and exit feasibility (its step 2), and the contractual terms that go beyond standard supplier clauses, with an internal acceptance record replacing the contract for open-source self-hosted models (its step 3).
- **Evidence has a defined quality order.** The [supplier security and privacy assurance standard](../../supply-chain/standard-supplier-security-and-privacy-assurance.md) sets a hierarchy of assurance evidence, preferring independent certification over self-declaration (its section 5). The hierarchy is a stated preference; the standard, not this page, defines which evidence tier is acceptable where.
- **Monitoring continues past onboarding and is trigger-aware.** The [supplier ongoing monitoring procedure](../../supply-chain/procedure-supplier-ongoing-monitoring.md) requires that defined events, such as a disclosed breach or a change of ownership, initiate an unscheduled review regardless of the scheduled cycle (its trigger-based reviews section). For AI suppliers, the AI due diligence procedure requires an out-of-cycle reassessment on triggers including a material change in the supplier's model architecture or data supply chain (its section 7.4).
- **Oversight extends past the first tier.** The [fourth-party and nth-party risk procedure](../../supply-chain/procedure-fourth-party-and-nth-party-risk.md) sets sub-tier visibility expectations scaled to supplier criticality, and defines the escalation conditions, including a supplier's refusal to disclose contractually required sub-tier information (its visibility tiers section and step 4). The [concentration risk register](../../supply-chain/register-concentration-risk.md) tracks where critical dependencies converge across dimensions including shared sub-tier providers and geography, and it feeds an annual supplier risk report at the governing-body level (its operating expectations); the umbrella standard separately requires a supply chain concentration risk report. The umbrella standard requires portfolio-level concentration visibility to be maintained by the executive role it names (its section 6.2).
- **Software composition is inventoried within a defined scope.** The [SBOM register](../../supply-chain/register-sbom.md) records the composition of built, purchased, and open-source software, with SBOM gaps escalated for contractual remediation at the supplier criticality tiers the register names (its operating expectations), and the [software composition analysis standard](../../dev-security/standard-software-composition-analysis.md) requires supply-chain attack mitigations and, for AI, provenance verification of pre-trained models, dataset licence review, and assessment of third-party LLM API dependencies (its sections 4.5 and 4.7).
- **AI supplier dependency is a governance input, not a footnote.** The [AI governance and risk framework](../../ai/framework-ai-governance-and-risk.md) carries supplier governance as one of its control domains, with external AI services reviewed for data handling, retention, training use, subcontracting, resilience, security, and exit (its control domains section), and its classification criteria include external model or supplier dependency as a factor an AI system should be classified on (its risk classification criteria; the corpus states this as a should).
- **The governing body's instrument is the question, and the answer is a report.** The [AI board oversight guide](../../ai/guide-ai-board-oversight.md) names structured interrogation as the governing body's primary oversight instrument (its section 5), lists governance red flags including responsibility attributed to a system or a vendor rather than an accountable person (its section 6), and describes what the governing body should expect to receive on a regular cadence (its section 7).

## What this means for the organization

The corpus position, read together, sets the governing body's third-party and AI supply-chain oversight as a small number of standing expectations. This reading is composite.

- **Require one programme, with AI inside it.** The corpus treats AI suppliers as a distinct class within the supplier programme, not a parallel track: the umbrella standard's due diligence domains include AI, and the AI-specific procedures extend, rather than replace, the general supplier lifecycle. Oversight that asks about the supplier programme and the AI supplier extensions together is asking the question the corpus is built to answer.
- **Expect evidence, not assurances.** Every lifecycle stage in the supplier framework is paired with an evidence class, and the assurance standard orders evidence by independence. A supplier relationship the organization cannot evidence at each stage is a relationship the corpus controls are not demonstrably operating on. Certification is evidence that a supplier's control environment was assessed; it is not, by itself, evidence that the organization's own lifecycle controls operate.
- **Treat visibility past the first tier as part of the ask.** The corpus states sub-tier visibility and concentration tracking as programme expectations with defined escalation, so "we have no visibility into our suppliers' own dependencies" is an unexamined answer rather than a settled one. The corpus scales the expectation to supplier criticality rather than requiring complete sub-tier knowledge, so the governing body's question is whether the expectation was applied, not whether every sub-tier is known.
- **Onboarding-only assurance is a weak answer.** The monitoring procedures make trigger-based reassessment a requirement, and the AI due diligence procedure adds AI-specific triggers such as a changed model architecture or data supply chain. An oversight answer that ends at "they passed due diligence" stops where the corpus keeps going.
- **Accountability held by a vendor is a red flag.** The oversight guide names responsibility attributed to the system or the vendor, rather than an accountable person, as a warning sign (its section 6). Accountability remains with the organization in the presence of supply-chain dependency.

## Evidence to request

Each item below is an evidence class the governing body can call for from management, and each is evidence for a named control or outcome.

- **The supplier risk classification and due diligence record** for a sample of critical suppliers, as evidence that the due diligence control in the [third-party and supply chain risk standard](../../risk/standard-third-party-and-supply-chain-risk.md) operated before onboarding (its section 4.1).
- **The completed AI vendor security questionnaire alongside the general supplier questionnaire** for AI suppliers, as evidence of AI-specific supplier assessment; the [AI vendor security questionnaire template](../../ai/template-ai-vendor-security-questionnaire.md) requires both to be submitted before contract signature (its operating expectations).
- **The contract control schedule** for critical and AI suppliers, as evidence that the minimum clauses in the [supplier security and privacy assurance standard](../../supply-chain/standard-supplier-security-and-privacy-assurance.md) (its section 6) and the AI provisions in the [third-party AI due diligence procedure](../../supply-chain/procedure-third-party-ai-due-diligence.md) (its section 6) are present.
- **The pre-engagement evaluation record and its recommendation, in the outcome set the procedure defines** for any foundation model in use, as evidence that the gate in the [foundation model lifecycle procedure](../../ai/procedure-foundation-model-lifecycle.md) operated (its step 2), including the exit-feasibility and substitutability outputs.
- **Monitoring records showing a trigger-based review that actually ran**, as evidence for the unscheduled-review control in the [supplier ongoing monitoring procedure](../../supply-chain/procedure-supplier-ongoing-monitoring.md) and the AI reassessment triggers in the [third-party AI due diligence procedure](../../supply-chain/procedure-third-party-ai-due-diligence.md) (its section 7.4).
- **The concentration risk register and the annual supplier risk report it feeds**, as evidence that portfolio-level concentration is tracked and reaches the governing-body level, per the [concentration risk register](../../supply-chain/register-concentration-risk.md) (its operating expectations) and the reporting table in the [third-party and supply chain risk standard](../../risk/standard-third-party-and-supply-chain-risk.md) (its section 10).
- **Sub-tier disclosure records for the suppliers the procedure's criticality model prioritizes**, as evidence for the visibility expectations in the [fourth-party and nth-party risk procedure](../../supply-chain/procedure-fourth-party-and-nth-party-risk.md), including any escalation where disclosure was refused (its step 4).
- **An extract of the SBOM register and its gap escalations**, as evidence for software supply-chain transparency per the [SBOM register](../../supply-chain/register-sbom.md), together with the AI dependency scan results the [software composition analysis standard](../../dev-security/standard-software-composition-analysis.md) requires (its section 4.7).
- **The exit and deletion evidence for a recently ended supplier relationship**, as evidence that the exit stage of the [supplier and cloud governance framework](../../supply-chain/framework-supplier-and-cloud-governance.md) operates, including the AI-specific exit expectations in the [third-party AI due diligence procedure](../../supply-chain/procedure-third-party-ai-due-diligence.md) (its section 8).

## Limitations

- This page creates no compliance and imposes no requirement. The linked corpus documents govern; where this page differs from a corpus document, the corpus document prevails.
- This page carries composite claims. The reading of the governing body's role as programme-level oversight, and the grouping of the corpus's supplier, AI, and software supply-chain documents into one oversight picture, are synthesized across the sources listed above, and the adopting organization validates that reading against its own governance model and committee structure.
- The corpus documents this page points to carry the specific tiers, thresholds, notification windows, retention periods, evidence hierarchies, cadences, and named roles and approval bodies. This page routes to them and does not reproduce them, so the corpus remains the single source for every value.
- Some corpus statements this page describes are stated in the corpus as should-strength expectations rather than requirements (among them the AI supplier assessment coverage list, the AI risk-classification criteria, and the reporting expectations in the oversight guide). This page does not upgrade them, and the corpus wording governs.
- This page makes no claim about how likely any supplier or supply-chain failure is, and none of its statements should be read as a probability or a frequency.
